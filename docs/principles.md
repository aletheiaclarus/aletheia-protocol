# The Clarus Protocol — Principles

Clarus is built around a small set of principles that should remain stable even as the system evolves.

## 1. Verify, Do Not Trust

Important public claims should be independently verifiable.

Clarus should not require citizens to trust:

- a government
- a political party
- a politician
- a company
- a database administrator
- Clarus itself

The system should make the underlying evidence available so that others can verify it.

## 2. Public by Default

Information concerning the use of public resources should be public by default.

This includes, where legally possible:

- budgets
- contracts
- payments
- public procurement
- public compensation
- project progress
- government commitments
- financial results

Exceptions should require a clear legal or security justification.

## 3. Privacy by Design

Transparency must not become mass surveillance.

Clarus should collect and expose only the minimum personal information necessary.

Personal information should not be placed on a public ledger when it is not necessary for verification.

Particularly sensitive information should remain protected.

## 4. Evidence Before Conclusions

Clarus should present evidence rather than conclusions.

An automated risk signal is not an accusation.

A statistical anomaly is not proof of corruption.

A missed promise is not automatically evidence of bad faith.

The system should show:

- the underlying data
- the source
- the rule or calculation
- the relevant dates
- uncertainty
- alternative explanations where available

People should be able to examine the evidence and reach their own conclusions.

## 5. Source Transparency

Every important record should identify where it came from.

Where possible, Clarus should preserve:

- the original source
- publication date
- retrieval date
- responsible institution
- dataset version
- relevant document
- cryptographic hash or integrity reference

The origin of information should be as transparent as the information itself.

## 6. Immutable History

Once a verified record has been published, changes should be visible.

Historical information should not silently disappear.

If a record is corrected, Clarus should preserve:

- the previous version
- the new version
- the reason for the change
- when the change occurred
- who or which institution submitted it

The goal is not to prevent corrections.

The goal is to prevent invisible corrections.

## 7. Independent Verification

No single organization should be able to determine what is true within Clarus.

Verification should be supported by multiple independent actors where practical, including:

- public institutions
- auditors
- researchers
- journalists
- civil society
- technical organizations
- citizens

Different parties should be able to reproduce important calculations from the underlying data.

## 8. Open Standards

Clarus should favor open and documented standards.

Data formats, APIs, verification methods, and protocol specifications should be publicly documented.

The system should avoid unnecessary dependence on a single proprietary technology or provider.

## 9. Open Source

Core Clarus software should be open source whenever legally and technically practical.

Anyone should be able to inspect the code, identify weaknesses, reproduce the system, and contribute improvements.

Security should come from sound design and verification, not from keeping the implementation secret.

## 10. Democratic Neutrality

Clarus is not a political party and should not favor a political ideology, candidate, government, or opposition group.

The protocol should provide the same verification standards regardless of who is in power.

The system should document facts and evidence without deciding what citizens should believe.

## 11. One Person, One Legitimate Vote

Where Clarus supports democratic voting systems, each eligible person should be able to exercise their legally defined voting right once.

Voting systems must simultaneously protect:

- eligibility
- uniqueness
- ballot secrecy
- auditability

A system should be able to demonstrate that a legitimate vote was counted without revealing how an individual voted.

## 12. Separation of Identity and Choice

A voter may need to prove that they are eligible to vote.

That does not mean the system should know which candidate or option they selected.

Identity verification and vote choice should therefore be separated wherever the electoral system permits it.

## 13. Technology Has Limits

Cryptography, blockchain, artificial intelligence, and software can strengthen verification.

They cannot guarantee that the original information is true.

A blockchain can protect the integrity of a record after publication.

It cannot prove that the government, company, or person who supplied the record was truthful.

Human and institutional verification therefore remain necessary.

## 14. Signals Are Not Verdicts

Automated systems may identify patterns that deserve attention.

They should never automatically declare a person or organization guilty.

Risk signals should lead to investigation, review, or requests for clarification.

The final determination should remain within the legally appropriate human and institutional process.

## 15. Right of Reply

When Clarus publishes a risk signal or identifies a potentially incorrect record, the affected institution or organization should have a documented opportunity to respond.

Responses should become part of the public evidence where legally appropriate.

Transparency should include both the original claim and the response.

## 16. Accountability Without Automatic Punishment

Clarus can make evidence easier to find.

It should not automatically replace:

- courts
- auditors
- legislatures
- electoral authorities
- constitutional procedures
- legally established disciplinary processes

The protocol provides evidence and verification.

Legitimate institutions remain responsible for decisions and consequences.

## 17. Equal Rules

The same verification standards should apply regardless of:

- political party
- country
- government
- individual
- company
- institution

A system designed for accountability loses credibility if its rules change depending on who is being examined.

## 18. Minimal Trust, Maximum Verifiability

The ultimate design goal is to minimize the amount of trust required between participants.

Where trust is unavoidable, Clarus should make it visible.

Where a claim can be verified mathematically, it should be.

Where it can be verified from public records, those records should be accessible.

Where verification is impossible, the limitation should be clearly stated.

> **Clarus does not promise perfect government.**
>
> **It proposes a system in which government can be examined more openly, consistently, and independently.**
